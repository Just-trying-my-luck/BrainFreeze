##EDWARDIAN PROFESSIONS AND WAGES

###Skilled & Unskilled Adult Men
Adult male artisans and laborers typically worked 54 to 60 hours over a 6-day week.

####Carpenter (Skilled Tradesman): 35s to 40s per week (Approx. £1.75 to £2.00). 
Carpenters were highly respected, skilled artisans. They earned a solid hourly rate of about 8d to 9d, allowing them to comfortably afford the basic food basket.

####Master Baker: 32s to 38s per week (Approx. £1.60 to £1.90). 
A master baker running a commercial kitchen or a large shop earned a comfortable working-class wage, though the hours were grueling (often starting at 2:00 AM or 3:00 AM).

####Labourer / Builder (Unskilled): 20s to 24s per week (Approx. £1.00 to £1.20). 
Unskilled construction laborers (carrying bricks, digging foundations) made roughly 4d to 5d an hour. This was right on the "poverty line"—if work stopped due to bad weather, the family went hungry.

###Shop & Domestic Workers (Women & Teens)
Women and young girls earned significantly less than men, often trapped in highly demanding service roles.

####Shop Worker (Adult / Teen): 10s to 15s per week for women; 20s to 28s per week for men. 
Shop assistants worked long, exhausting 14-hour days standing on their feet. Some shops operated under the "living-in" system, deducting money from these wages for cramped, shared beds upstairs.

####Tea Shop Worker (Young Girl): 7s to 10s per week. 
Popularized by chains like Lyons Tea Shops, young girls working as waitresses (frequently called "Nippy" girls slightly later in the era) earned very low base wages and had to rely heavily on small tips to survive.

####Scullery Maid (Domestic Work): £10 to £12 per year (Roughly 4s to 4s 6d per week equivalent). 
While this sounds shockingly low, a scullery maid—the absolute lowest rank in the household hierarchy—received free room and board. Her accommodation, coal, and food were fully paid for, meaning her small cash wage was pure profit (which was almost always sent straight back to her parents).

### Child Laborers & Youth "Street Trades"
By 1900, the Elementary Education Act required children to stay in school until age 12, but many worked part-time before/after school, or full-time immediately after turning 12.

####Lamp Lighter (Child / Youth):
5s to 7s per week. While adults held the official city contracts, young boys were often hired to run ahead with long poles to light and extinguish incandescent gas street lamps at dawn and dusk.

####Newspaper Boy: 4s to 6s per week. 
Newsboys (or "newsies") bought bundles of papers from printing presses with their own money and hawked them on street corners. Their income entirely depended on how many papers they successfully sold; a rainy day meant devastating losses.

####Knocker-up (Child / Teen): 2s to 4s per week. 
Before affordable alarm clocks, "knocker-ups" used long bamboo sticks or pea-shooters to tap on bedroom windows to wake up factory workers for the early shift. Children often did this early-morning route to secure extra money before heading to school.

####Chimney Sweep (Child): No direct wage 
(Earned food and a place to sleep). Note on historical context: Climbing boys (children forced up chimneys) were technically made illegal by acts in 1840 and 1875. However, the law was widely flouted in poorer districts well into the late Victorian and early Edwardian periods. Poor children were "apprenticed" to a Master Sweep. The Master took all the cash profits for cleaning the chimneys and reselling the soot; the child was only given basic food, a floor to sleep on, and a fresh set of clothes once a year.
